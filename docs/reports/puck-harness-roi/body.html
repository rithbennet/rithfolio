2. Output per dollar, version by version

Every harness change bent the curve upward: v3 closed 31 rows for $542, while night 1 closed 3 for $277. Part of that is smaller tasks, but the overhead per row also halved.

3. Where the time went

Reviews, not building, set the pace: a median task spent 18 minutes with its builder and 23 with reviewers, inside 85 minutes of wall-clock. The harness's real win on time is that it ran unattended: 24 rows merged in 11 hours, overnight and into the morning, while you sent no messages.

The lost time is mostly Sol's: 13 BLOCKED and 6 BAD_TEST verdicts, where the code was never judged because the test store hit its rate limit, a sign-in failed, the preview browser dropped or the store was in the wrong state. A Sol run holds the test store for 15 to 25 minutes, so every wasted one held up the queue behind it.

How much you had to step in

You sent 71 messages in all: 19 while researching the spec on 6 Oct, 29 while redesigning the harness on 8 and 9 Oct, and 23 to the orchestrator threads. Of those 23, the last 10 were new product direction (the design gallery, Publish all, the shop page, Release 6), not unblocking. The orchestrators asked 18 questions through the harness, each with a default, and carried on with independent work while they waited.

Context management costs time too. Harness v3 hands off to a fresh thread at about 200k tokens: 17 threads in 17 hours, each rebuilding its context from the ledger and the start file. That rebuild is $64 of cache writes, 36% of v3's orchestrator cost. It is still cheaper than one long thread: 9 Oct's single thread grew to 963k tokens and cost $15.36 of orchestration per row; v3 cost $5.66.

4. What the checking caught

Reviews found real defects most of the time: Astra failed 25 of 32 first reviews, and about 86 of its and Sol's serious findings were genuine code defects. The builders' own receipts (tests, typecheck, lint, one self-review) did not catch them.

To judge how many findings were real, I read a random sample of 25 serious findings. Astra's were genuine defects 13 times in 14 (the other asked for evidence that was missing). Sol's were genuine 6 times in 11; the rest were rate limits, a missing rebase and a snapshot timeout. Scaled up, that is about 86 real defects caught before merge estimate, of which 63 came from first reviews alone. What got past the checks

Reviewers check the code against the spec. They did not catch what the spec got wrong, and a few things slipped to staging:

  • Two layout gaps on the pilot store, found by Sol's staging verification (F-02 round 4): a doubled heading on lookbook pages and the Contact form in the wrong place. Both are rows in R5 now. Open
  • Product sense. You found that kits and templates were two menus for one idea, that their previews were text only, and that an apply could not be undone. No reviewer flagged these; they became R5's design gallery and Undo apply.

5. Would it be cheaper without the harness?

In API dollars, yes: builders alone come to about $351, a quarter of what the harness spent. In what you actually pay, no: the plans are flat. The trade is roughly $480 of reviews and fix rounds against about 86 defects you would otherwise find yourself.

What the numbers leave out is where the harness pays for itself:

  • A defect caught in the loop costs about $8: $4.25 of checking, a $1.17 fix agent and a $2.30 re-review, with the context still loaded. The same defect found on staging costs a cold session, your time to reproduce it and, for the severe third, a broken store for a seller.
  • Your hours are the scarce resource, not tokens. On a flat plan the marginal token is free until the weekly cap. What the harness buys is that the 24 rows merged in that overnight stretch needed nothing from you.
  • The harness itself cost $59 to build (postmortems, the night-1 report, v1 to v3) plus your 29 messages. That is paid once, and R5, R6 and a later general harness reuse it.

6. What your plans buy

Your $300 a month buys about $2,887 of API-priced work a week, roughly 40 times its price. At v3's pace that runs Claude for about 5.6 days a week but Codex for only 1.1, so Codex is now the limit.

The upgrade to Max 20x paid off at once. On 9 Oct, still on Max 5x, the orchestrator and builders hit the Claude session limit 18 times; in v3 on Max 20x, not once. On Max 5x the Puck run alone would have used 102% of the weekly allowance since 8 Oct; on Max 20x it is 26%.

7. Where to trim

Three changes would cut about a quarter of the spend without dropping a check, and one would stretch Codex.

  1. Run the orchestrator on Opus 5.5, not Fable 5.1.The orchestrator is 33% of all spend and its job is now mostly running puck-next and recording events. Opus would cost $225 for the same tokens, saving $221, and frees Fable's separate weekly cap. Keep Fable for writing a release's contract or the end report.
  2. Hold Astra to the rule you already wrote: risky rows only.Astra reviewed 27 of the 36 tasks merged since R2, though the ledger reserves it for auth, money, concurrent writes, data changes and contracts. Routine block tasks can go to Sol at a fifth of Astra's token price, saving about $135 estimate and making Codex last about 1.6 times as long.
  3. Fix Sol's environment once, not per run.19 verdicts and 955 agent-minutes went to rate limits, sign-ins, a missing rebase and store state. A test brand exempt from the rate limit, a session file that preflight proves, and a rebase check in puck-ready would remove most of them.
  4. Keep the release review and the 200k relay.The release review caught the drop-zone bug that made pages unsavable, and the relay cut orchestration per row from $15.36 to $5.66.
  5. Size Codex to the work, or move some reviews to Claude.Codex lasts about 1.1 days a week at this pace against Claude's 5.6. Either the $200 ChatGPT Pro tier, four times this allowance, or an Opus reviewer for routine rows on the larger Claude allowance.
